A complete guide to realistic website development timelines and how to stay on schedule.

Realistic website build timelines — 1-page site to full e-commerce — so you can plan launch dates, avoid surprises, and know when to expect results.

Introduction: Why Website Timelines Always Feel Wrong

If you've ever commissioned a website, you've probably experienced this: the developer says "4 weeks," and three months later you're still waiting. You're not alone. According to a 2025 survey by Standish Group, over 66% of web development projects finish late or over budget. The reasons range from unclear requirements to mid-project scope changes, but the result is always the same — frustration, lost revenue, and missed launch dates.

The problem isn't that developers are slow. It's that most clients don't know what a realistic timeline looks like, what each phase actually involves, or what they can do to keep the project on track. This guide gives you the full picture: real timelines for every type of website, what happens during each phase, and the specific things that speed up or slow down a build.

Whether you're launching a simple business site, building a custom web application, or setting up an e-commerce store, you'll walk away with a clear expectation of when your website will be ready — and what you need to do to make that happen.

Website Build Timelines by Project Type

Not all websites take the same amount of time to build. A single landing page and a custom e-commerce platform are fundamentally different projects. Here's a realistic breakdown of timelines for every common project type:

Project Type Timeline Key Considerations
Single Landing Page 3–7 days Template-based, minimal custom design, no CMS needed
5-Page Business Website 2–4 weeks Home, About, Services, Blog, Contact — standard layout with custom design
10–20 Page Brochure Site 4–8 weeks Multiple service pages, case studies, team bios, detailed content
Custom Web Application 8–16 weeks User dashboards, APIs, databases, real-time features, authentication
E-commerce Store (Shopify/WooCommerce) 4–8 weeks Product catalog, payment gateway, shipping, inventory management
Custom E-commerce Platform 12–24 weeks Custom checkout, unique business logic, third-party integrations
Ongoing Redesign (10+ pages) 6–12 weeks Existing site redesign, content migration, preserving SEO, redirects

These timelines assume a professional development team working full-time on your project with clear requirements. If the developer is juggling multiple clients or you're working with a freelancer who has other projects, expect the calendar time to be longer even if the active work hours stay the same.

What Happens During Each Phase

Every professional website project follows the same basic phases, though the time spent on each varies. Understanding what happens in each phase helps you know where your time is going and what you need to do as a client to keep things moving.

Discovery & Planning (1–2 weeks)

This is where the foundation gets laid. The developer or agency learns about your business, your goals, your audience, and your competitors. Key deliverables during this phase include:

  • Requirements document: a clear list of every feature, page, and function the website needs
  • Sitemap: a visual map of all pages and how they connect
  • Wireframes: basic layouts showing where content, images, and calls-to-action go on each page
  • Technical specifications: hosting platform, CMS choice, third-party integrations, and performance requirements

This phase often takes longer than expected because it requires input from you. If you can't clearly describe what you want, the discovery phase will stretch as the developer asks follow-up questions. Having a detailed brief or reference sites you like can cut this phase in half.

Design (1–3 weeks)

Once the wireframes are approved, the designer creates visual mockups — usually starting with the homepage, then applying the design system to inner pages. You'll typically see 1–2 rounds of revisions before the design gets locked in.

  • Homepage mockup: the first design you see — this sets the visual direction for the entire site
  • Inner page templates: how service pages, blog posts, and contact pages will look
  • Mobile design: ensuring the design works on phones and tablets, not just desktop
  • Design approval: once you sign off, the developer starts building

The design phase is where most delays happen. If you take more than 3–5 days to review mockups, the entire project timeline shifts. And if you request more than 2 rounds of revisions, you're adding weeks. The best approach: gather all feedback in one document, send it in a single message, and make all your requests at once rather than trickling them in over several days.

Development (2–6 weeks)

This is the longest phase. The developer takes the approved design and turns it into a functioning website. What happens here depends heavily on the project type:

  • CMS setup: installing and configuring WordPress, Shopify, or your chosen platform
  • Frontend development: building the HTML, CSS, and JavaScript that makes the site work in browsers
  • Backend development: server-side logic, databases, user authentication, and dynamic features
  • Content integration: adding your text, images, videos, and other content to the site
  • Third-party integrations: payment gateways, email marketing, analytics, CRM systems

For a simple 5-page site, development might take 2 weeks. For a custom web application with user accounts and API integrations, it can easily take 6–16 weeks. The complexity of your features is the single biggest factor in how long development takes.

Content Creation (1–4 weeks)

Content is often the silent killer of website timelines. Many projects stall here because the client hasn't prepared their copy, images, or other assets. Content creation includes:

  • Website copy: professional writing for each page, optimized for SEO and conversions
  • Photography and images: professional photos, stock images, or custom illustrations
  • SEO content: meta titles, descriptions, alt text, and keyword-optimized page content
  • Multimedia: videos, animations, infographics, or interactive elements

If you hire a professional copywriter, expect 1–2 weeks for a standard business site. If you're writing the content yourself, it often takes longer because it falls to the bottom of your daily priority list. The best strategy: write or commission all content before development starts, so the developer can integrate it as they build.

Testing & QA (1–2 weeks)

Before launch, every page and feature needs to be tested across multiple conditions:

  • Cross-browser testing: Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge — and older versions where your audience uses them
  • Mobile responsiveness: testing on various screen sizes and devices
  • Page speed: optimizing images, code, and server response times for fast loading
  • Form testing: contact forms, newsletter signups, checkout processes — every interactive element
  • SEO audit: checking meta tags, sitemaps, robots.txt, internal linking, and structured data
  • Security review: SSL certificates, input validation, and vulnerability scanning

Rushing this phase is risky. A bug that makes it to launch can damage your reputation, hurt your SEO, or lose you customers. Budget at least a full week for thorough testing, even for simple sites.

Launch (2–5 days)

The final phase involves moving the site from the development environment to your live domain:

  • DNS configuration: pointing your domain to the new hosting server
  • SSL installation: setting up the HTTPS certificate for security
  • Final checks: verifying every link, form, and page works on the live domain
  • Redirects: setting up 301 redirects if you're redesigning an existing site
  • Analytics setup: installing Google Analytics, Search Console, and tracking codes

Don't rush the launch. A common mistake is deploying on a Friday afternoon and discovering Monday morning that the contact form doesn't work or the site loads slowly on mobile. Launch on a weekday morning when you and your developer are both available to address any issues quickly.

Factors That Speed Up a Website Build

Some projects finish ahead of schedule. Here's what makes that happen:

  • Clear requirements from day one. When the developer knows exactly what to build, there's no guessing or rework. A detailed project brief with specific features, examples, and content saves weeks.
  • Fast approvals. Clients who review mockups within 24–48 hours and give consolidated feedback keep the project moving. The best clients send one detailed feedback document instead of five separate emails.
  • Prepared content. Having all your text, images, logos, and brand guidelines ready before the project starts means the developer never has to wait for content.
  • Using templates and frameworks. Starting with a proven theme or framework instead of building from scratch cuts development time by 30–50%. This is one reason WordPress sites launch faster than fully custom builds.
  • Decisive decision-making. When you know what you want, you don't spend two weeks debating color schemes or navigation layouts. Strong opinions, loosely held, keep projects on track.
  • Single point of contact. Having one person who makes all decisions prevents conflicting feedback from multiple stakeholders that creates back-and-forth delays.

Factors That Slow Down a Website Build

These are the timeline killers that turn a 4-week project into a 4-month ordeal:

  • Scope creep. The number one timeline killer. "Can you also add a booking system?" "What about a membership area?" Every new feature added mid-project resets the timeline. A change that seems small to you might require days of backend work.
  • Delayed feedback. When the developer sends mockups and waits five days for a response, that's five days the project sits idle. Over a full project, slow approvals can add 2–4 weeks.
  • Missing content. Developers can't build pages without content. If the copywriter hasn't finished or you haven't provided product descriptions, the project stalls. This is the most common delay in website projects.
  • Changing requirements. "Actually, let's use a different platform." "Can we change the entire navigation?" Mid-project changes don't just add time — they often require tearing down what was already built and starting over.
  • Complex integrations. Connecting to third-party APIs, payment processors, or legacy systems takes longer than expected. If the third-party documentation is poor or the API behaves unexpectedly, it can add weeks of troubleshooting.
  • Multiple stakeholders. When five people all have different opinions about the design, you'll spend more time in meetings than in production. Design by committee is the enemy of deadlines.

How to Get Your Website Built Faster

You don't need to be a developer to keep your website project on schedule. Here are seven practical steps you can take as a client:

  1. Write a detailed project brief before contacting any developer. Include your goals, target audience, required features, pages needed, and examples of sites you like. This single document can save you a week of back-and-forth.
  2. Prepare all content before the project starts. Write or commission your website copy, gather your photos, and finalize your logo and brand assets. Handing over complete content on day one prevents the most common type of delay.
  3. Set a clear approval process. Decide who in your company reviews mockups and give that person authority to make decisions. A single approver with clear preferences is 5x faster than a committee.
  4. Respond to developer requests within 24 hours. Even a quick "Got it, I'll review this tonight" message keeps the momentum. Silence kills timelines.
  5. Stick to the agreed scope. If you think of a new feature mid-project, write it down for a future phase. Adding it now means restarting parts of the project.
  6. Use a proven platform when possible. WordPress with a quality theme, Shopify, or Webflow can launch in half the time of a fully custom build — and they're perfectly sufficient for most businesses.
  7. Trust your developer's timeline. Professional developers pad their estimates for a reason. When they say 4 weeks, they mean 4 weeks of actual work. If you push for "2 weeks instead," you'll get a rushed product that costs more to fix later.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to build a 5-page website?

A standard 5-page business website typically takes 2 to 4 weeks to build. This includes discovery, design, development, content creation, and testing. If you have all your content ready and provide fast approvals, it can be closer to 2 weeks. If content is still being written or approvals are delayed, expect closer to 4 weeks or more.

How long does it take to build an e-commerce website?

An e-commerce website built on Shopify or WooCommerce typically takes 4 to 8 weeks. A custom e-commerce platform with unique features, payment gateways, and inventory management can take 12 to 24 weeks. The timeline depends on the number of products, payment integrations, shipping rules, and design complexity.

Can a website be built in 24 hours?

A very basic landing page can be built in 24 hours using templates or website builders. However, a professional business website with custom design, proper SEO, responsive layout, and testing requires significantly more time. Rushing a build often results in poor quality, security issues, and missing functionality that costs more to fix later.

How long does web design take after development starts?

The design phase typically takes 1 to 3 weeks after discovery is complete. This includes initial mockups, client review, revisions, and final approval. Complex designs with custom illustrations or animations may take longer. Getting design approval right the first time is one of the biggest factors in keeping the overall project on schedule.

What causes website projects to take longer than planned?

The most common causes of delays are scope creep (adding new features mid-project), slow client feedback and approvals, missing or incomplete content, changing requirements after development has started, and complex third-party integrations. Clear planning, prepared content, and timely communication can prevent most of these delays.
